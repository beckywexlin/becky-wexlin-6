Does what you wear change how you act? The research on enclothed cognition

August 3, 2026·11 min read

Everyone has one: the shirt you put on when you need to be a slightly different person that day. The question is whether that's a real psychological effect or a story you tell yourself while getting dressed. There is a genuine scientific literature on this, it has a name — enclothed cognition — and it went through a public near-death experience that makes the surviving evidence a lot more interesting than the original hype.

Here's the honest version, including the part where the famous study didn't hold up.

The idea, and the problem with it

The term was coined in a 2012 paper by Hajo Adam and Adam Galinsky. The proposal: clothes systematically influence the wearer's feelings, thoughts and behaviours through their symbolic meaning — and crucially, that it takes both the meaning and the physical act of wearing the thing. The famous demonstration involved a white coat: described as a doctor's coat, it improved attention on a test; described as a painter's coat, the same garment did nothing.

It was an irresistible finding. The paper has been cited more than 600 times and covered in more than 160 news outlets.1 It launched a thousand "dress for success" articles and a fair amount of "dopamine dressing" content.

Then a high-powered replication attempt failed to reproduce one of the original effects — the standard 2010s psychology story. Which raised the obvious question: is any of this real?

The 2025 audit, and what survived

In 2025, a paper in Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin did the thing that should always follow a failed replication: it audited the whole literature. The authors ran z-curve and meta-analyses across 105 effects from 40 studies in 24 articles, totalling 3,789 participants.1

The results "raise concerns about the replicability of early enclothed cognition studies but affirm the evidential value for effects published after 2015." The authors attribute the split to the marked improvement in psychological research practices in the mid-2010s — bigger samples, pre-registration, less flexibility in analysis.

Their conclusion: the later studies "support the core principle of enclothed cognition — what we wear influences how we think, feel, and act."

Horton, Adam & Galinsky, Pers Soc Psychol Bull, 2025 (PMID 37458322)

One caveat we'll flag ourselves, because it's the sort of thing that should be disclosed: two of the three authors of this audit — Adam and Galinsky — are the original authors of the 2012 paper. That doesn't invalidate a z-curve analysis, which is a fairly mechanical statistical procedure, but it's not a fully disinterested review either. Read the primary source if you care about the details.

The upshot is a narrower and more believable claim than the 2012 hype: the effect appears to be real, the early evidence for it was weak, and the good evidence is recent. So let's look at the recent stuff.

The uniform experiments, which are genuinely unsettling

The strongest post-2015 work uses uniforms, because a uniform has an unambiguous symbolic load.

A 2023 study in Frontiers in Psychology put 40 participants in civilian clothes, military uniforms, and Red Cross uniforms, then paired them up in a task where one participant could administer real electric shocks to another for a small monetary reward — either freely, or under instruction. They measured prosocial behaviour, sense of agency, and neural empathic response via EEG.2

Wearing the Red Cross uniform produced more prosocial behaviour than civilian clothing, and a stronger neural response to witnessing another person's pain.

Wearing the military uniform increased participants' sense of agency when acting freely.

Pech & Caspar, Frontiers in Psychology, 2023 (PMID 37854147)

Note what that rules out: it isn't "wearing a uniform makes you compliant" or "formal clothes make you focused." Two uniforms, opposite symbolic content, different effects — including a measurable difference in the brain's response to someone else's pain. The meaning is doing the work, not the garment.

The less comfortable result came in 2020, in Psychological Reports. Participants were randomly assigned to wear a police uniform or not, then completed a first-person "Shooter Task" simulation requiring them to shoot or not shoot targets holding either guns or ordinary objects.3

Participants randomly assigned to the police uniform were more likely to shoot unarmed targets than controls — regardless of the target's race. The effect was partially moderated by participants' pre-existing attitudes: those who justified police use of power shot innocent targets more often than those who were wary of it.

Mendoza & Parks-Stamm, Psychological Reports, 2020 (PMID 31272294)

Random assignment, so this is causal within the experiment. That's a long way from "wearing black makes you feel powerful," and it's a reminder that enclothed cognition is not an inherently cheerful idea.

The wristwatch study, and the limits of the evidence

Not everything in this literature is experimental, and it's worth seeing what the weaker end looks like so you can calibrate.

A 2015 PeerJ paper compared Big Five personality traits between people who habitually wear a wristwatch and people who don't, in an exploratory sample of over 100 and a confirmatory sample of over 600. Watch wearers scored significantly higher on conscientiousness. A third study (n = 85) found watch wearers arrived at appointments significantly earlier than controls.4

Interesting — but note the design. Nobody was randomly assigned a watch. Conscientious people plausibly buy watches; the watch needn't be doing anything to them. The authors discuss it in relation to enclothed cognition, but it's correlational, and it can't distinguish "the object changes you" from "the object reveals you."

That distinction matters for the t-shirt question, and we don't want to blur it: most of what a graphic tee does is probably revealing rather than transforming. The shirt is a signal you're choosing to send. Whether it also loops back and changes you is the enclothed cognition claim, and it's supported for high-symbolic-load garments in controlled settings — not demonstrated for a novelty tee.

Why the old shirt does something the new one doesn't

There's a second literature that explains a thing the enclothed cognition research doesn't: why a beaten-up shirt you've had for eight years feels different from an identical new one.

That's nostalgia, and it turns out to be one of the better-studied emotions in social psychology. A 2011 paper in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology reported six studies on it.5

Nostalgia was positively associated with a sense of meaning in life, and experimentally increased it. The link was mediated by feelings of social connectedness. Threatened meaning increased nostalgia; nostalgia in turn reduced defensiveness following a meaning threat, and disrupted the link between meaning deficits and compromised psychological well-being.

Routledge et al., J Pers Soc Psychol, 2011 (PMID 21787094)

A more recent review concludes that nostalgia helps people find, maintain and restore meaning, with knock-on benefits for well-being.6

The mediation result is the good part: nostalgia doesn't work by making you wistful about objects. It works by making you feel connected to people. Which is exactly why the shirt from the concert, the trip, the era, the person — carries weight that a new shirt can't fake. The garment is a retrieval cue for a social memory.

It's also, we'd argue, the honest explanation for the entire nostalgia-driven apparel market: the Y2K revival, meme shirts pulling from a decade ago, the whole business of selling people a reference they already have feelings about. It's not fake sentiment. There's a mechanism.

So what does this license, and what doesn't it?

It doesn't license: "wear this shirt and become confident." No study supports a graphic tee changing your personality. The demonstrated effects use garments with heavy, unambiguous institutional meaning — a doctor's coat, a Red Cross vest, a police uniform — under experimental conditions.

It also doesn't license the reverse dismissal — the "it all failed to replicate, clothes are just fabric" line. The 2025 audit is fairly clear that post-2015 effects hold evidential value.

What it does support: the mechanism is symbolic meaning, and symbolic meaning is not assigned by the manufacturer. It's assigned by you. The white coat did nothing when it was a painter's coat. Same object, different meaning, different result. Which means the shirt that works is the one whose meaning you actually endorse — not the one that's objectively coolest.

That's a more useful design principle than "dress for success," and it happens to be the argument for buying weird things you specifically like rather than whatever's selling. A shirt that says something true about you has symbolic load. A shirt you bought because it was trending has none, which is why it ends up at the bottom of the drawer.

Common questions

Is enclothed cognition debunked?
No, but it was overstated. Early studies are of questionable replicability; post-2015 effects hold up under meta-analysis.1

Does dressing up actually make you perform better?
The evidence supports effects from garments with strong symbolic meaning to the wearer, in controlled tasks. Generalising that to "wear a blazer, get a promotion" goes well past the data.

Why do I feel different in certain clothes?
The theory's answer is symbolic meaning plus physical wearing. The nostalgia literature adds a second route: garments tied to a past period cue social connectedness, which supports a sense of meaning.5

Does this mean a funny t-shirt changes my mood?
Unstudied. What is documented is the effect of the laughter a funny shirt might cause in other people — which we covered separately in the science of the funny t-shirt.

The bottom line

The strongest version of this research isn't "clothes make the man." It's something narrower and stranger: a garment can change behaviour when it carries a meaning the wearer recognises, and the same physical object with a different meaning does nothing at all.

Which puts the burden back on you. The shirt doesn't come with meaning installed. You supply it — and the ones you supply the most meaning to are the ones you'll still be wearing in five years.

The white coat only worked when it was a doctor's coat. Same fabric, same buttons, different story. The story was the active ingredient.

Why your t-shirt makes you itch: dyes, resins, and what the research actually shows

August 3, 2026·11 min read

You buy a shirt. You put it on straight out of the mailer because you're excited. Two hours later your neck is red and you've decided you're "allergic to cheap cotton." You're probably not. The dermatology literature is fairly clear that when clothing causes a rash, the fibre is usually innocent — it's the chemistry applied to the fibre that does it.

We sell shirts, so we have an obvious interest here. But we also spend a lot of time thinking about blanks and finishes, and the peer-reviewed research on this turned out to be more specific and more useful than the usual internet advice. Here's what's actually in it, cited, with the practical version at the end.

Before anything else: this is a summary of published research for general interest, not medical advice. If you have a persistent or severe skin reaction, see a dermatologist — and read the section below on what to ask them, because it makes a measurable difference.

The main culprit has a name: disperse dyes

Textile-related allergic contact dermatitis has a leading suspect, and it's consistent across decades of the literature. A 2022 study in Contact Dermatitis opens by stating it plainly: disperse dyes "are the most prevalent causes of textile-related allergic contact dermatitis."1

The detail that matters for shopping is what disperse dyes are for. They're used to colour synthetic fibres — polyester, acrylic, acetate and polyamide.1 They exist because synthetic fibres are hydrophobic and won't take the water-soluble dyes that work on cotton. So the single highest-yield thing you can know about textile dye allergy is that the classic offenders live on synthetics.

Two specific dyes come up repeatedly as the worst offenders. Hatch and Maibach's review in the Journal of the American Academy of Dermatology identified Disperse Blue 106 and Disperse Blue 124 as "the strongest clothing dye sensitizers to date."2 A 2023 Dutch study found Disperse Orange 3 and Disperse Blue 106 were the most common individual dyes producing positive patch tests.3

How common is this, honestly?

Worth being precise, because "toxic clothing" content on the internet is wildly overstated and we're not going to add to it.

In the general dermatitis population: a Swedish study patch tested 1,481 dermatitis patients with the standard textile dye mix. 3.6% reacted.4

In people already suspected of textile dye allergy: a Dutch study of 209 such patients found 25.8% tested positive to the dye mix or an individual textile dye. Of those, about half had a reaction judged clinically relevant.3

So: not an epidemic, not vanishingly rare. A small but real slice of people have a genuine allergic sensitivity to textile dyes, and a much larger group experiences ordinary irritation that isn't allergy at all.

The older literature suggests it's under-detected rather than over-detected. Hatch and Maibach concluded that "textile dye sensitivity is more common than previously believed," and — importantly — that standard screening patch test series were inadequate to find it.2 That warning from 1995 has held up well; more on it below.

The second culprit: formaldehyde resins

The other well-documented cause is formaldehyde-based resin finishes. These have been applied to fabric since the 1920s to create permanent-press and wrinkle-free properties, and they're documented as potent sensitizers in susceptible people.5 Formaldehyde's role as a textile allergen is established enough to have its own review literature.6

This is where the practical advice gets easy: "wrinkle-free," "permanent press," "no-iron" and "easy care" are the marketing words for the resin finish. A plain cotton jersey tee generally has no reason to carry one. A dress shirt engineered not to wrinkle almost certainly does.

The wash-before-you-wear question, with actual numbers

Everybody's mother said to wash new clothes before wearing them. It turns out someone measured it.

A 2013 study in the Journal of Toxicology and Environmental Health tested 20 clothing articles for formaldehyde content, then took the worst offenders and washed them the way a normal person would.7

17 of the 20 items had no detectable formaldehyde at all. But one shirt came in at 3,172 ppm, and a pair of pants at 1,391 ppm — the two highest results were up to 40 times greater than international textile regulations allow.

Washing and drying those items using ordinary consumer methods reduced formaldehyde to between 26% and 72% of untreated controls. Water temperature and wash type produced no clear trend — hot, cold, hand, machine, it didn't much matter. Hot ironing had noticeably less effect than washing.

Novick et al., J Toxicol Environ Health A, 2013 (PMID 24053365)

Read that carefully, because both halves matter. Most clothing is fine — 85% of the sample had nothing detectable. But the distribution has a nasty tail, you can't tell which garment you're holding, and one wash cuts the exposure substantially. The cost of washing a new shirt is one wash. Just do it.

It also reframes what washing is for. If you've read our guide to caring for printed graphic tees, you know the first wash matters for the print. It matters for your skin too.

What to actually ask a dermatologist

This is the most useful thing in the whole literature and almost nobody knows it.

Standard baseline patch test series — the default panel most patients get — are not sufficient to detect textile dye sensitivity. Hatch and Maibach said so in 1995 and recommended dedicated textile dye patch test series.2 The 2023 Dutch study quantified exactly how much gets missed:

Testing individual textile dyes in addition to the standard textile dye mix caught patients the mix alone would have missed. Without the individual dyes, 46.3% of dye-positive patients and 35.7% of patients with a clinically relevant reaction would have been missed entirely.

Nijman et al., Contact Dermatitis, 2023 (PMID 36461774)

So if you genuinely suspect your clothes, the specific ask is: "can we patch test with a textile dye series, including individual dyes, not just the baseline series?" A negative baseline test does not rule textile dye allergy out.

One more finding worth knowing: people who react to the textile dye mix also react to extracts taken from real synthetic garments that contain none of the pure dyes in the test mix.1 Meaning the standard panel is a proxy, not a complete map. The authors were explicit that more work is needed to identify the actual culprit chemicals in real clothing.

The fibre question, and an honest complication

Given all of the above, "choose natural fibres" looks like straightforwardly good advice for dye exposure, and for that specific purpose it mostly is: disperse dyes go on synthetics, so a cotton tee sidesteps the best-documented sensitizer class. That's a real reason we print on heavyweight cotton blanks rather than poly blends — though we'd be lying if we said skin chemistry was the original motivation. It was the hand-feel and the print quality. The dye thing is a bonus we learned about later.

But cotton isn't a magic word, and there's a complication worth stating because it cuts against the tidy story.

A 2021 study in PLOS ONE ran 37 consumer apparel textiles through five domestic laundry cycles to measure microfibre shedding.8 Mechanically-treated polyester — fleeces and jerseys — shed roughly six times more than woven nylon. Fair enough, that's the expected result. But:

"Interestingly, cotton and wool textiles also shed large amounts of microfibers (165 ± 44 mg kg⁻¹ per wash)" — a figure comparable to the mechanically-treated polyester samples (161 ± 173 mg kg⁻¹).

The study found a greater than 850-fold difference between the lowest- and highest-shedding textiles, and that consumer laundry lint traps retained up to 90% of polyester fibres.

Vassilenko et al., PLOS ONE, 2021 (PMID 34242234)

Cotton fibres aren't plastic, so the environmental implication differs. But "natural fibre" is not a free pass on shedding, and anyone telling you cotton solves everything is simplifying. Construction quality turned out to matter more than fibre category — an 850-fold spread is enormous, and it's mostly about how the fabric is built.

Which is, conveniently, the same thing that determines whether a shirt survives two years of wearing. We went deep on that in what makes a quality graphic tee and the Comfort Colors vs Bella+Canvas comparison.

The practical version

If you only take five things from all of this:

1. Wash new clothes before wearing them. One wash cuts formaldehyde content substantially, and temperature doesn't matter, so there's no excuse.

2. Treat "wrinkle-free" and "permanent press" as ingredient labels. Those finishes are the formaldehyde resins in the literature. A plain cotton tee shouldn't have one.

3. If you react to clothes, suspect the synthetics first. Disperse dyes are the most prevalent documented cause, and they live on polyester, acrylic, acetate and polyamide.

4. Buy from brands that name their fibre content and their blanks. Not because named blanks are hypoallergenic — they aren't — but because a brand that won't tell you what a shirt is made of also isn't telling you what's been applied to it. Same argument we made in how to spot a well-made graphic tee.

5. If you're patch tested, ask for the textile dye series specifically. The baseline panel misses a substantial share of real cases.

None of this requires panic. The honest summary of the literature is that most clothing is chemically unremarkable, a minority of garments carry meaningful residues, a small percentage of people are genuinely sensitised, and one wash plus some skepticism about "easy care" finishes handles most of the risk.

Most clothing is fine. But 17 of 20 items testing clean means three didn't — and you can't tell by looking. Wash the shirt.
